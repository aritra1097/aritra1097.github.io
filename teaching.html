Teaching

In middle school in India, I struggled with math. A high school teacher changed that by approaching things differently. He would start with the big-picture idea of why we were doing something, often relating it to history and to things around us. He would then break complicated problems into smaller, manageable components. But the final push was his encouragement: he was the first teacher who believed in me and my ability to do math long before I did. That was when math stopped being a subject I got through and became a way of testing what everyone around me felt sure of, and it eventually led me to study economics.

In my own classroom, I try to build a similar sense of comfort, with a central emphasis on encouraging curiosity: my students question theories and ideas, including their deeply held beliefs, and learn how to think critically about them.

In my classroom

My research on issues of identity makes me acutely aware that students have diverse backgrounds and lived experiences and face different challenges. I focus on connecting with my students early on, encouraging individual in-person meetings.

When I introduce any technique, we discuss the objective, often break the problem into smaller components, and then solve it together. For anything that involves math or mathematical intuition, I lean away from slides and encourage students to write along with me as I write on the board.

My lectures are structured around multiple activities, starting with simple exercises or games intended to ensure broad participation. Often, students begin these activities individually and then discuss their responses with randomly assigned group members. The random assignment helps students meet peers they would not usually approach.
